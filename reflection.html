Critical Reflection — Deciphering Big Data

What happened?

During this module I studied data collection, cleaning, modelling, database management, regulatory compliance, APIs and emerging analytics. The most significant event in my learning was changing my proposed database project. My Unit 6 plan focused on Collateralised Loan Obligations (CLOs) in a custodian-bank environment. I discovered that the size and complexity of the proposed data made preparation and implementation difficult for me. I decided independently to use a more manageable sample of the Brazilian Olist E-Commerce Dataset. The revised project was completed as an individual submission, rather than through a project group.

This decision was important because it changed the business question while preserving the core technical learning objectives: design related tables, prepare data, check integrity and extract useful information. It also showed that a feasible, validated implementation can be more educational than an ambitious design that cannot be completed reliably.

So what did I learn?

Initially, the banking proposal seemed relevant and professionally interesting. When the data became difficult to manage, I felt uncertain about how to progress. I now recognise that I had concentrated too heavily on the potential value of the banking application and not enough on the resources required to process its data. Changing direction was a practical decision, but it also required accepting that the original domain-specific objectives would not be achieved.

The Olist work provided evidence of progress. I structured customers, orders, products and order items in a relational database, used primary and foreign keys and checked referential integrity. The reported foreign-key check returned no violations. In the later executive summary, Python profiling identified 36 missing cells in four descriptive product fields and nine validation checks were reported as passing. These results increased my confidence in systematic checking, but they also taught me not to confuse a successful technical check with proof that the entire analysis is complete or unbiased.

The SQL findings were useful: the selected orders generated merchandise sales of 135,150.38, and the analysis identified differences by category, customer state and month. Yet the sample contained only 1,000 delivered orders and was not randomly selected. Excluding other order statuses could distort the picture. I therefore learned to separate accurate calculations within a sample from claims about the full business. This distinction is central to responsible analytics.

I also learned that data preparation requires more judgement than I initially expected. Removing import errors, choosing data types, preserving keys and deciding how to handle missing descriptions all affect later conclusions. For example, postal-code prefixes should be stored as text if leading zeros are meaningful. Small design choices can influence whether records remain usable and whether results can be reproduced.

Collaboration and individual contribution

The collaborative discussions offered a different kind of learning. In the first discussion I examined IoT data collection and responded to peers about data quality and cybersecurity. In the second, I compared the GDPR with the UAE Personal Data Protection Law and discussed pseudonymisation, minimisation, access controls and risk assessment. These exchanges encouraged me to evaluate alternatives and support my arguments rather than merely repeat the original post.

My database project was completed independently because I was unable to participate in a project group for that assessment. I was responsible for the preparation, relational design, SQL development, validation, analysis and reporting. Accordingly, I cannot provide genuine peer-contribution ratings for a group that did not exist. This is a limitation in demonstrating the teamwork learning outcome, although the module discussions provide evidence of interaction with other students. I should not present independent project work as group collaboration.

AI-assisted tools supported selected design, SQL and troubleshooting tasks. Their value was speed and the ability to suggest possible approaches; their weakness was the risk of plausible but inaccurate outputs. I therefore tested queries and database relationships instead of relying on suggestions alone. I now see verification as part of responsible use of AI, not as an optional final step.

Now what will I do differently?

For future projects, I will begin with a short feasibility assessment: examine dataset size, column types, missingness, identifiers, legal sensitivity and available computing resources. I will define a minimum viable database and test it on a representative subset before expanding the scope. I will also document assumptions and preserve evidence of changes to the plan.

Technically, I intend to practise more Python/Pandas automation, improve SQL query design, work with larger datasets in stages and test transaction recovery rather than discussing it only theoretically. For analysis, I will distinguish descriptive findings from predictive claims and explicitly report selection bias and uncertainty.

Professionally, I want to become more confident in explaining why a technical decision was made, not just describing what I implemented. The project taught me that independent judgement includes recognising limits and adapting responsibly. My main achievement was not changing to an easier dataset in itself; it was turning a difficult starting point into a completed, validated learning exercise while recognising what the final results could and could not establish.
